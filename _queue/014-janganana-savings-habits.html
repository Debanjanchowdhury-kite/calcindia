Janganana 2026: What Census Data Says About How India Saves

Published ·7 min read

Janganana — the Hindi word for census — captures far more than just population counts. The Census of India portal is the official source for this and past census rounds, and combined with household survey data from bodies like NSSO and RBI's own household savings reports, it paints a detailed picture of how Indian families actually manage money. And the picture that emerges is a little surprising: despite years of financial-literacy campaigns and a genuine SIP boom in the news, the overwhelming majority of Indian household savings still sit in just two places — bank deposits and physical gold.

Where India's Household Savings Actually Sit

Broad estimates from household finance surveys consistently show a pattern that hasn't shifted dramatically in years:

Savings InstrumentApprox. Share of Household Financial + Physical Savings
Bank deposits (savings + FD)~40-45%
Gold & jewellery~15-20%
Real estate~15-20%
Insurance (traditional plans)~8-10%
Mutual funds / equity (incl. SIPs)~5-8%
PPF, EPF, NPS & other retirement schemes~5-7%

Mutual funds and direct equity — the instruments that have historically delivered the highest inflation-beating returns over long horizons — remain a small slice of the pie despite the SIP industry's rapid growth in absolute numbers. The number of active SIP accounts has grown sharply in recent years, but as a share of India's roughly 250+ million income-tax-paying-eligible households, participation is still a minority behaviour.

Why the Gap Exists

  • Familiarity and trust. Bank deposits and gold have multi-generational trust; mutual funds are a newer, less-understood category for most households outside major cities.
  • Perceived safety over real returns. An FD "feels" safe because the number never goes down, even though after tax and inflation it frequently delivers a near-zero or negative real return — see the exact math in our SIP vs Fixed Deposit comparison.
  • Gold's cultural and liquidity role. Gold isn't purely an investment in Indian households — it's dowry, insurance-of-last-resort, and a store of value that can be pawned quickly, roles a mutual fund folio doesn't fill.
  • Distribution reach. A bank branch or gold jeweller exists in nearly every town; a mutual fund distributor or financial advisor often doesn't, though digital platforms are closing this gap quickly.

The Cost of the Gap, in Real Numbers

The difference between "safe" and "high-return" savings compounds dramatically over decades. A household saving ₹10,000/month purely in bank FDs at roughly 7% nominal (closer to 4-5% after tax for most earners) accumulates very differently from the same ₹10,000/month split toward equity SIPs at a long-term historical average closer to 12%:

Strategy (₹10,000/month, 20 years)Approx. Final Corpus
100% Bank FD (~7% nominal)~₹52 lakh
100% Equity SIP (~12% historical avg.)~₹99 lakh

Neither extreme is necessarily the "right" answer for every household — FDs and gold serve genuine safety and liquidity roles a family shouldn't abandon entirely. But the census-and-survey data suggests most Indian households are more heavily weighted toward the safe, lower-return end than their actual risk capacity and time horizon would justify.

Closing the Gap in Your Own Household

  1. Keep 3-6 months of expenses in liquid FDs or savings — this part of the "safe" allocation is genuinely correct and shouldn't move.
  2. Direct new long-term savings (10+ year horizon) toward SIPs rather than accumulating more FDs — model the difference in the SIP calculator.
  3. Don't abandon PPF and EPF — they remain excellent tax-free, low-risk anchors for retirement savings; see how EPF, NPS and PPF compare.
  4. Start small if trust is the barrier. A ₹500/month SIP costs less than most households' monthly gold-purchase habit and is a low-stakes way to build comfort with the instrument — our ₹500/month SIP breakdown shows exactly what that grows into.
The takeaway: Janganana and household survey data don't just count people — they quietly reveal that most Indian families are still under-invested in the instruments most likely to build long-term wealth. Closing that gap doesn't require abandoning FDs or gold; it just requires directing your next rupee of long-term savings a little differently than the national average currently does.
